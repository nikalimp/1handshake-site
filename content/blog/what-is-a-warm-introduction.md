**A warm introduction is when a person who knows both of you connects you with someone you want to meet.** Instead of a cold message from a stranger, the other side gets a note from someone they already trust: “I know this person, I think you should talk.” The trust between the two of them carries over to you — that is the whole trick.

## Warm vs cold

A cold message arrives with no context. The receiver has to decide, in a few seconds, whether you are worth their time, and most of the time the safe answer is to ignore it. A warm intro arrives with three things a cold message never has:

- **a reason** — the mutual friend explains why the two of you should talk;
- **a reference** — someone the receiver knows has already met you;
- **a small social cost of ignoring it** — saying no to a friend's intro feels different from deleting spam.

That is why founders ask for warm intros to investors, why hiring managers prefer referrals, and why the best collaborations often start with “you two should meet.”

## Why warm intros are hard to get

The people who could introduce you are usually there. The problem is that you can't see them. Your contacts are spread across messengers, email and social networks, and nobody keeps a map of who knows whom. So you either guess and ask around, or you give up and send a cold message.

Professional networks were supposed to fix this, but they mixed real relationships with connections between strangers. When a list of contacts includes people you have never met, “a mutual connection” stops meaning “someone who can vouch for you.”

## How to ask for an intro

A good intro request makes the friend's job easy and keeps everyone's time in mind.

1. **Ask the right person.** Pick someone who actually knows the person you want to meet — not just someone connected to them online.
2. **Explain why in two sentences.** Who you are, what you want to talk about and why it is useful for the other side, not only for you.
3. **Make it easy to forward.** Write a short note the friend can send as is. They should not have to rewrite your pitch.
4. **Ask first, then intro.** A considerate friend checks with the other person before connecting you (a “double opt-in”). Don't take it personally if the answer is no.
5. **Close the loop.** Thank the friend and tell them how the conversation went. People who see their intros work are happy to make more.

## Making the next intro easier

The best way to get intros is to make them: connect people you know when you see a good match. And keep your own information easy to share — a short description of who you are, what you do, what you need and how you can help. When someone wants to introduce you, they should be able to send one link.

## Short answers

**Is a warm intro the same as a referral?** A referral is a kind of warm intro, usually for a job. A warm intro can be for anything: a cofounder, an investor, a client, advice.

**What if I don't know anyone who knows them?** Look one step further: the people your friends know. That is usually enough — most of the people you need are one handshake away.

**Should I ask for many intros at once?** No. Ask for one at a time, and only when you have a clear reason. Your friends' trust is the currency here.
